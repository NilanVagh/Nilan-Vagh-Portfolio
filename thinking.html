Part 1

The problem with systems of record

Every tool in this portfolio starts from the same diagnosis: the systems People functions rely on were designed to store what happened, not to govern how it gets decided.

Systems of record — HRISes, performance platforms, compensation tools — are excellent at the last step: capturing the outcome. They have no visibility into, and no influence over, everything that came before it. Who was in the room. What information the decision-maker had in front of them. Whether the right approvals were sought, in the right order. Whether exceptions were handled consistently or ignored.

The result is a function that generates high-quality data about past decisions and low-quality infrastructure for current ones. Rating distributions are visible; whether the rating process was consistent across managers is not. Compensation ranges exist; whether decisions were made with those ranges in front of the decision-maker is unknowable. Headcount data is current; whether the headcount request was routed correctly is anyone's guess.

This is not a data quality problem. It is a decision infrastructure problem. And it doesn't get solved by upgrading the system of record. It gets solved by building the layer that sits between the process and the record — the layer that governs how decisions actually get made. That is what each tool in this portfolio is designed to be.

Part 2

The shift to decision infrastructure

Decision infrastructure is the governed layer between workforce intent and workforce outcome. It's not the policy. It's not the system. It's the logic that routes decisions to the right people, surfaces the right information at the right moment, enforces the right guardrails, and creates a legible audit trail of what happened and why.

When you design a People process as a decision infrastructure problem, the questions change. Instead of asking "what steps does this process have?" you ask:

What decision is actually being made here?
Who has the right to make it — and at what threshold does that change?
What information does the decision-maker need in front of them at the moment of decision?
What are the guardrails — the conditions under which the decision cannot proceed?
What exceptions exist, who handles them, and how are they tracked?
What is the audit trail — not just what was decided, but who decided, when, with what information?

These questions don't get answered by buying better software. They get answered by design — by making the decision logic explicit, building it into the workflow, and governing it over time.

The tools in this portfolio are each an answer to these questions for a specific workforce decision domain. Not features. Not automations. Governed decision infrastructure — where the logic is the product.

The design process behind every build
01
Design Thinking
Start with the real problem — not the process as documented, but how the decision actually gets made in practice. Who's involved, where it breaks, what gets lost between submission and outcome. Understand the failure modes before designing the solution.
02
Governance by Design
Map the decision rights, guardrails, exception paths, and audit requirements before building anything. Governance isn't a layer added on top — it's built into the workflow from the start. The structure of the tool reflects the structure of the decision.
03
Self-Service with the Right Information
Self-service only works when the person using it has exactly what they need — no more, no less. A manager levelling a role needs to see comparable roles and salary bands at the point of decision, not navigate to a separate framework document. A hiring manager planning comp needs to see their budget envelope and cohort distribution in real time, not discover an overspend at consolidation. Self-service without the right information is just friction with fewer emails.
Part 3

The Orchestration Test

A practical test for identifying where decision infrastructure should exist — and where it shouldn't.

The Orchestration Test
Ask of any People process:
Administrative

Is the work primarily about recording an outcome? Keep it administrative. Automate where possible. The goal is accuracy and efficiency — not workflow redesign.

Governed Workflow

Is the work primarily about getting the right decision made — routing information, enforcing guardrails, managing exceptions, creating accountability? Redesign it as a governed workflow. The goal is auditability and consistency, not just throughput.

Most People processes contain both elements. The Orchestration Test is about identifying which element is primary — and designing accordingly. The mistake is treating everything like a system-of-record transaction.

Performance calibration is a governed workflow. The system of record can hold the final rating — it cannot support the weighted curves, manager-level visibility controls, promotion guardrails, and exception routing that make calibration defensible. That gap is why the Calibration Engine exists.

Remuneration review is a governed workflow. A spreadsheet can hold a number — it cannot enforce budget envelopes, surface compa-ratio at point of decision, or flag cohort outliers before submission. That gap is why the Rem Review Planning Studio exists.

A promotion record is administrative. A promotion decision — who reviews it, what criteria apply, what approval chain governs it, what happens when criteria aren't met — is a governed workflow. Same domain. Entirely different design requirements.

Part 4

Key concepts

The vocabulary behind the tools. Split into two groups: system properties (what a governed workflow has) and design approach (how it gets built).

The vocabulary of People Infrastructure. These concepts appear throughout the builds.

Workflow State

The explicit representation of where a decision sits in a governed process — initiated, under review, pending approval, approved, exception, closed. State makes workflow visible. Without it, every process is a black box between submission and outcome.

Decision Rights Architecture

The explicit mapping of who can make which decisions under which conditions. Not an org chart — a governance layer. Who approves a promotion at L4 vs L6? What happens when budget authority is exceeded? Who can override a guardrail, and what does that create in the audit log?

Governance by Design

Building compliance into the workflow itself, rather than auditing for it after the fact. Guardrails that prevent decisions from proceeding when criteria aren't met. Approval gates that can't be skipped. Audit trails that are a natural byproduct of the process, not a separate activity.

Human-in-the-Loop Systems

Decision infrastructure that keeps humans in authority over judgment calls while automating the surrounding work — routing, data surfacing, guardrail enforcement, exception flagging. AI-assist where it's useful. Human decision where it matters. Explicit about which is which.

Role-Based Visibility

Information surfaces that show people exactly what they need and nothing they shouldn't have. Not a dashboard for everyone — a visibility layer designed around decision roles. The executive sees cross-divisional distribution. The manager sees their cohort. The People team sees exceptions and outliers.

Exception Management

The deliberate design of what happens when a decision falls outside normal parameters. Not an error state — a workflow state. Who is notified? Who reviews? What documentation is required? What is the audit entry? Well-designed exception management is often what separates a governed process from an administrative one.

Part 5

AI readiness requires infrastructure first

Most organisations try to jump straight to AI-enabled operations. The problem is everything they need underneath it doesn't exist yet.

AI doesn't solve fragmented operations by itself. Without workflow architecture, governance, and orchestration already in place, AI simply inherits and scales the same coordination problems. The plumbing matters more than the prompt.

The maturity model below shows where most organisations actually are versus where AI-enabled operations require them to be. Levels 1 through 3 are infrastructure. They have to exist before Level 4 or Level 5 is worth attempting.

AI Readiness Maturity Model — People Infrastructure
What AI should do in People workflows
  • Suggest, summarise, compare, route, and detect signals
  • Generate context before a decision — not after
  • Execute bounded, low-risk operational tasks within defined parameters
  • Flag anomalies and surface patterns that would otherwise be missed
What AI should not do
  • Bypass workflow controls or operate without governance
  • Replace approval accountability or make high-stakes decisions autonomously
  • Obscure the audit trail — every AI action should be logged and reversible
  • Be deployed without an owner, defined scope, escalation triggers, and rollback path
On agentic execution

Agents are execution actors operating within defined boundaries — not autonomous decision owners. Treat them like operational workers: create and approve them, configure their scope, monitor their output, improve them, retire them when they're no longer needed. The governance layer has to exist first. A bounded agent running inside a governed workflow is powerful. An agent running outside one is just a faster way to make the same mistakes.

The cross-functional reality

Most People workflows are not purely People workflows. They are cross-functional enterprise workflows. A new hire requires IT provisioning, payroll setup, finance budget alignment, and manager onboarding — not just an HRIS record. An internal move requires Finance for compensation changes, Legal for contract variations, and IT for access updates. An EOR-to-entity conversion requires Finance, Legal, and Business sign-off — not just a People recommendation.

This is why orchestration matters. And why agent execution in People is constrained unless there is end-to-end system access, permissions design, and cross-functional ownership clarity. The tools in this portfolio are designed with those dependencies explicit — not assumed away.

Part 7

Vendor tools are built around the default process. Yours probably isn't.

Your process probably isn't median. That gap is structural — and it doesn't close by picking a better vendor.

Enterprise HRIS platforms and performance tools are genuinely good at what they're designed for. They capture records reliably, run standard review cycles, and handle the processes that most organisations run the same way. But they're built around the default process — the way most companies of a certain size run calibration, or compensation review, or job levelling. The more your process diverges from that median, the less the tool fits.

This isn't a criticism of those platforms. It's a structural reality. A vendor tool cannot know that your ANZ calibration is more complex than your US calibration. It cannot know that your performance management process needs weighted distribution curves rather than a simple bell curve. It cannot know that your promotion guardrails need to account for specific eligibility criteria that reflect your employment law context. Those aren't configuration options. They're context — and context is the one thing vendors can't ship.

Onyx runs two regions. The US team uses Lattice's native calibration — it fits their process. The ANZ team uses a custom-built Calibration Engine — Lattice's calibration doesn't fit theirs. Same company. Same tool available. Different answer.

The implication isn't "build everything." Most processes should use vendor tools — they're faster to deploy, cheaper to maintain, and good enough for standard workflows. The question is whether your process is standard. For People teams, the honest answer is: the processes that matter most — calibration, compensation planning, levelling, internal mobility — are almost never standard. They reflect the organisation's specific culture, employment law context, workforce composition, and decision-making norms. That's where the gap opens up.

And this is also why custom building requires a different kind of investment than most People teams expect. The technical build is the easier part. The harder part is the design thinking that has to happen first: What does the governance layer actually need to do? What are the exception paths? Who sees what, at what stage? What happens when the process breaks? Those questions can only be answered by someone who has run the process, in organisations like yours, and knows from experience what breaks and why. The tools in this portfolio exist because that experience informed the design — not just the build.

Part 8

Bring the tools where work happens

The most governed workflow is useless if nobody uses it.

Most People tools make the same mistake: they expect employees and leaders to come to the system. Log into the HRIS. Open the performance platform. Navigate to the planning tool. The system is the destination.

That model made sense when systems were scarce. It doesn't make sense when people are already working in Slack, in email, in the tools their teams actually use every day. Asking a manager to open a separate tool to action a levelling decision or complete a calibration triage is adding friction to the exact moment you need the least friction.

The better design principle: bring the governed workflow to where the work already happens. Not as a notification. As a surface where the decision can actually be made.

A Slack message that says "your triage is due" is a notification.
A Slack message with an inline form that lets you complete the triage and submit it back into the governed workflow — that's infrastructure.

This is the direction the tools in this portfolio are heading. Some of it is already built — Slack notifications when decisions are needed, email alerts when thresholds are crossed. The deeper integration — forms inside Slack, decisions actioned without leaving the channel — requires engineering partnership. That's not a limitation of the thinking. It's an honest account of what a People team can build alone versus what needs a data and engineering team to scale.

01
Tool works standalone
The governed workflow is built and functional. Logic, guardrails, audit trail. This is the proof — and it exists now.
02
Tool notifies where work happens
Slack and email alerts when thresholds are crossed, decisions are needed, or exceptions are flagged. Partially built across the current tools. Buildable by a People team with GAS.
03
Tool operates inside where work happens
Manager types /level-role in Slack. A governed form appears. The decision is made, logged, and routed — without leaving the channel. This is the vision. It needs Engineering and Data partnership to scale. That's not a weakness — it's the right boundary between what People builds and what People commissions.

The better platforms are moving in this direction. Lattice has Slack notifications and is building toward in-workflow actions. Workday has the integration infrastructure but the surface is still largely system-first. The gap between "sends a notification" and "lets you action the decision inside Slack" is still wide — and it's where internally built tools can move faster than vendors.
